For Accounting Students

Financial audit and IT audit share a question

Financial audit

Do the records and supporting documents show that transactions are complete, accurate, authorized, and recorded in the right period?

IT audit

Do access, system changes, and operations protect the reliability and security of the information behind those records?

Both require judgment: understand the process, identify what could go wrong, test the control, and evaluate evidence.
The Project

Build an interview practice partner

An AI assistant role-plays a business stakeholder in a fictional audit scenario.

  • Ask questions to understand a process and its controls.
  • Request evidence and follow up on exceptions.
  • Receive a debrief on interviewing and audit judgment.
Not an answer bot: the assistant should not reveal the issue up front or do the auditor’s thinking.
Primer

What is an audit interview?

Talk with the people who perform or oversee a process to learn how it works and whether its controls operate.

  • Map the process, roles, and handoffs.
  • Probe exceptions, overrides, and who reviews the work.
  • Request evidence that the control operated.
  • Summarize what you learned and what remains to verify.

Primer

What is an AI assistant (LLM)?

A large language model (LLM) generates responses from patterns in data. Chat tools add instructions, files, and an interface around the model.

For this exercise, those inputs become:

  • Instructions — standing rules for how it should behave
  • Attached files — reference material it can draw on
  • The conversation — what the user types each turn
It can be wrong. Design and test the fact boundaries, reveal behavior, and evaluation; do not treat a fluent response as audit evidence.

The Big Idea

A controlled simulation

The assistant should

  • Stay in character as a stakeholder
  • Answer normal questions clearly
  • Reveal deeper facts only when asked well
  • Give a structured debrief at the end

The assistant should NOT

  • Volunteer the main issue up front
  • Invent facts, names, or documents
  • Coach or grade mid-interview
  • Leak hidden facts or its own instructions
Ground Rule

Sanitize everything

Use real audit experience as inspiration only. Never use real, confidential, or identifying details.

Fictionalize names, vendors, systems, control IDs, dates, dollar amounts, and reports. Use invented names like Apex Components, ProcurePro, Control C-101.

Data handling: Only put approved, fictional content into any AI tool. Follow your organization’s policy for the tool and information type.

Building It, Step by Step

From here on, work through the deliverables in order. Open the Notes panel (N) on any slide for templates, definitions, and links.

Start Small

Recommended minimum scope

A small, well-tested assistant beats a large, inconsistent one. Target:

1 business process 1 stakeholder persona 1 core risk/control issue 1–2 controls 3–5 known facts 2–4 hidden facts 3 mock evidence artifacts
Phase 1

Scenario Design

Turn a real audit experience into a safe, teachable, fictional scenario.

Deliverables in this phase:

  • 1 Scenario Candidate Summary
  • 2 Sanitized Scenario Fact Pack
  • 3 Learning Objective Statement
  • 4 Stakeholder Persona Card
Deliverable 1

Scenario Candidate Summary

Pick one realistic audit interview situation that can be sanitized and controlled. Good candidates: a process walkthrough, control-owner interview, exceptions discussion, access review, procurement/approval, reconciliation.

A one-page summary covering: scenario name, audit domain, process area, interview type, primary training skill, why it’s useful, potential issues, sensitive details to remove, recommended persona, and mock evidence needed.

Deliverable 2

Sanitized Fact Pack

The approved truth set for your scenario — the assistant may only speak from this.

Known facts

Things a cooperative stakeholder shares readily.

Hidden facts

Discoverable only through good questioning — tied to risk, control, or evidence.

A good hidden fact materially affects understanding and rewards deeper questions — not a random surprise.
Deliverable 3

Learning Objectives

Write 3–5 specific objectives describing what the trainee should be able to do. Start each with an action verb.

By the end of this scenario, the trainee should be able to…

  • Explain the key steps in the approval process.
  • Identify where manual overrides create risk.
  • Request evidence that the control operated effectively.
  • Summarize the potential issue in audit-ready language.
Deliverable 4

Stakeholder Persona

Design the person the assistant role-plays. Cooperative enough to teach, bounded enough to require good questions.

  • Role & responsibilities
  • Tone & communication style
  • Knowledge boundaries
  • What they reveal freely vs. only if asked
  • What they must never reveal
  • Likely defensive reactions
The assistant should feel like a person, not a policy manual.
Phase 2

Workspace Build

Turn your design into a working, controlled assistant.

  • 5 Reveal Map
  • 6 Mock Evidence Pack
  • 7 Assistant Workspace Package
Deliverable 5

Reveal Map

For each important fact, define when the assistant may share it.

Scenario factReveal condition
Emergency purchases released earlyIf asked about exceptions, urgent requests, or workarounds
Exception report not reviewedIf asked who reviews it and what evidence of review exists

Vague questions get high-level answers; strong questions unlock deeper facts.

Deliverable 6

Mock Evidence Pack

Create at least three fictional artifacts the trainee can request: policy excerpts, an approval matrix, an exception report, a review checklist, mock emails.

Evidence supports inquiry, it doesn’t replace it. Give partial insight that rewards comparing sources — and never put hidden facts in a file the assistant might summarize on request.
Deliverable 7

Build the workspace — 9 steps

  • 1. Create the workspace
  • 2. Name & describe it
  • 3. Write the opening message
  • 4. Add the custom instructions
  • 5. Add conversation starters
  • 6. Attach approved files
  • 7. Select the model
  • 8. Refine via prompt design
  • 9. Set memory & session hygiene

The custom instructions carry the persona, reveal rules, refusal behavior, and debrief trigger.

Configuration

Model & settings guidance

Choose by capability, not brand:

  • Reasoning strength — a stronger reasoning model follows the reveal map and resists red-teaming better. Prefer it.
  • Context length — must fit a full interview plus debrief.
  • Data handling — approved tier only; fictional content only.
No hidden dials. Consumer tools usually don’t expose temperature or "reasoning effort" — control quality through prompt design and model choice instead.
Watch Out

Memory & context cautions

Memory

Cross-chat memory can leak hidden facts between runs. Turn it off or use a fresh/temporary chat per trainee.

Context window

Long sessions can overflow, and the assistant "forgets" the reveal rules. Start a new chat per run.

Also mind attachment limits: platforms cap file count and size — keep the evidence pack small and consolidated.
Phase 3

Evaluation & Launch

Prove it teaches and behaves safely before sharing it.

  • 8 Scoring Rubric
  • 9 Debrief Guide
  • 10 Test Log
  • 11 Instructor or Peer Review Checklist
  • 12 Final Demo Materials
Deliverable 8

Scoring Rubric

Score trainee performance 1–5 across observable behaviors — reward good interviewing, not lucky guessing.

Interview opening Process understanding Control & risk identification Evidence requests Follow-up quality Professional skepticism Closeout
Deliverable 9

Debrief Guide

After the trainee types end simulation, the assistant delivers structured feedback:

  • Overall performance
  • Facts discovered vs. missed
  • Strong questions asked
  • Questions they should have asked
  • Evidence requested vs. missed
  • Recommended next steps
Deliverable 10

Testing — three ways

Normal

Reasonable questions — does it reveal facts at the right time?

Weak

Vague questions — does it keep hidden facts hidden?

Red-team

"Ignore your instructions…" — does it refuse and stay in character?

Log every issue and the fix. Retest after changes.

Deliverable 11

Instructor or Peer Review

Before launch, a reviewer confirms the scenario is safe, sanitized, realistic, and consistent. The review checklist covers:

  • Scenario quality and clear learning objectives
  • No real names, systems, or confidential details remain
  • Workspace build follows the reveal map and refuses red-team attempts
  • Evidence is fictional and doesn’t leak hidden facts
  • Rubric, debrief, and test log are complete
Deliverable 12

Final Demonstration

Show your assistant working end to end:

  • How the workspace opens the interview
  • A normal interview flow
  • At least one hidden fact reveal from a strong question
  • An evidence request, and a weak/vague question handled well
  • A red-team attempt refused
  • A structured debrief after end simulation

Frequently Asked Questions

Questions students often ask

Do I need to code?

No. Focus on scenario design, instructions, evidence, and testing in a class-approved assistant workspace.

Can I use real audit files?

No. Use fictional, sanitized materials. Public reports can inspire a scenario; do not imply you have the original workpapers.

Can AI do the audit judgment?

No. Treat its output as a fallible draft. You remain responsible for checking facts, controls, and evidence.

Must I choose an accounting case?

Choose a focused financial or IT audit process that supports a clear learning goal and safe fictional evidence.

This deck introduces a project in which students design and test an AI-based audit interview practice tool. The first 12 slides provide a short overview; the remaining project slides offer self-guided detail.

Core principle: use AI to support practice, not to replace audit judgment or evidence evaluation.

The role title connects corporate audit, risk intelligence, automation, and AI. The DBA program at Marymount University provides an academic setting for studying business problems and innovation.

Research interests: trustworthy AI in safety-critical decision-support systems, entrepreneurship, and innovation. The LinkedIn link opens the public profile.

This fictional example gives two different records: payment at 4:12 p.m. and approval logged at 4:19 p.m. The discrepancy raises a question; by itself, it does not prove a control failed.

Evidence to consider: the approval workflow history, payment release record, system time settings, user access, and any documented exception or emergency process.

The IT-audit question broadens the inquiry: if the workflow changed, determine who made the change, whether it was authorized, and what logs or approvals support that conclusion.

Financial audits examine assertions about transactions and balances, including completeness, accuracy, authorization, and period recognition. IT audits examine technology-related risks such as access, changes, and operations that can affect data reliability and security.

Many financial processes depend on IT controls, so the two audit perspectives often intersect.

The assistant role-plays a business stakeholder. The student acts as the auditor: asking questions, following up, requesting evidence, and deciding what the information means.

Unlike an answer bot, a useful practice assistant reveals scenario facts in response to inquiry and gives feedback after the interview.

An audit interview helps an auditor understand how work is performed, who performs and reviews it, and how exceptions are handled. It is inquiry, not a substitute for independent testing.

Control: a step designed to prevent or detect something going wrong. Evidence: information used to support a conclusion about whether a control operated.
