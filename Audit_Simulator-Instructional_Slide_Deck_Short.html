Your future AI overlord just approved a $48,000 payment.

It approved it seven minutes after the money went out. Its explanation: “The ledger balances. You’re welcome.”

Payment sent · 4:12 p.m.Approval logged · 4:19 p.m.

Accounting question

Was the payment properly authorized—and what evidence would establish the timeline?

IT audit question

Who configured the approval workflow, and was the configuration change authorized?

Future overlord or not: don’t accept a confident explanation. Ask for evidence.
Executive Summary

What, why, and what we’re asking you to do

What we’ll cover

Audit interviewing, public case examples, and a small AI assistant that role-plays a stakeholder.

Why it matters

Practice skepticism, control thinking, and evidence requests while learning where AI can help—and fail.

Your assignment

In a team, design one focused, fictional audit interview; build and test the AI practice partner; present what you learned.

The goal is not to make AI the auditor. The goal is to become a better auditor using AI thoughtfully.

Why Accounting Students Should Care

Financial records depend on people, controls, and systems

Financial audit

Ask whether transactions and balances are complete, accurate, authorized, and recorded in the correct period.

IT audit

Ask whether access, system changes, and operations protect the reliability and security of financial information.

Shared audit habit: understand what could go wrong, identify the control, and evaluate evidence—not just explanations.

The Practice Partner

Build an AI stakeholder—not an AI answer key

The assistant does

  • Role-play one business stakeholder.
  • Answer from a bounded, fictional fact pack.
  • Reveal details in response to relevant follow-up questions.

The student auditor does

  • Map the process and identify risks.
  • Ask questions and request evidence.
  • Decide what to verify and explain why it matters.
A fluent AI response is not audit evidence. The simulation rewards good inquiry, not lucky guessing.
Three-Step Build

Keep the project small, then test it

  1. Design
    Choose one process, learning goal, stakeholder, and a few known/hidden facts.
  2. Build
    Write reveal rules, create fictional evidence, and configure the assistant workspace.
  3. Evaluate
    Test normal, vague, and adversarial questions; revise and demonstrate the results.

One process · one stakeholder · one key risk · a few testable facts.

Trust & Safety

Use AI without handing it the keys

Protect information

Use class-approved tools and fictional, sanitized materials only.

Check the output

Test for invented facts, leaked information, and inconsistent responses.

Keep human judgment

Students remain responsible for evaluating evidence and drawing conclusions.

Do not use confidential workpapers, real names, live systems, or sensitive business details.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers

Do I need to code?

No. The work is scenario design, instructions, evidence, and testing in a class-approved assistant workspace.

Can I use real audit files?

No. Use fictionalized content. Public reports can inspire your scenario, but they are not permission to reproduce protected material.

What if the AI is wrong?

That is what testing is for. Record the error, improve the instructions or facts, and retest.

Can AI make the audit judgment?

No. The assistant is a practice stakeholder; the student evaluates the evidence and makes the judgment.

This is a made-up example for discussion, not a report of a real transaction. The payment is timestamped before its approval, but the timestamps alone do not prove why or how this happened.

Possible evidence includes the approval history, payment release record, system time configuration, user access, and documented exception procedures. Ask what each item can establish—and what it cannot.

The “AI overlord” line is a joke about over-trusting automated decisions. The point is human accountability and evidence, not a claim that AI runs an audit function.

What: a short tour of audit interviewing, public audit examples, and a student-built AI stakeholder simulation.

Why: audit work depends on effective inquiry, control understanding, and evidence evaluation; these skills also help people assess AI outputs critically.

Ask: teams create a focused, fictional scenario, build and test the practice partner, then demonstrate what they learned.

Financial audits often examine assertions such as completeness, accuracy, authorization, and cutoff. IT audits examine controls around access, system changes, and operations.

Financial reporting depends on technology, so an IT control weakness can affect financial information. Interviews help understand the process, but corroboration and testing are still needed.

Tampa Bay Office Furniture: the ISACA teaching case covers system access, program changes, and computer operations. The available link is a third-party transcription of the 2011 case, assembled from 25 audit notes; individual underlying logs and interview transcripts are not supplied separately. Attribute ISACA when using it.

Peace Corps purchase cards: the 2009 OIG report addresses policy, cardholder oversight, approvals, monitoring, and risk assessment. It describes document and transaction analysis, interviews, and observations.

FBI Trilogy: GAO-06-306 (2006) covers contractor invoice review, questioned costs, equipment purchases, and asset accountability. The report describes interviews, walkthroughs, data analysis, and physical asset work.

These public sources contain reported findings, not complete working-paper files. Use them for background and attribution; create fictional training materials.

The assistant is a role-play partner, not a source of authoritative audit facts. Its permitted knowledge should come from the scenario fact pack and evidence artifacts.

Students should control what is known and what is revealed through explicit instructions and a reveal map. Evaluate its responses against the designed facts.

Design: one process and one stakeholder keep the simulation manageable. Identify a specific skill, such as probing an exception or requesting evidence of review.

Build: known facts establish the scene; hidden facts reward relevant follow-up questions. Mock evidence should agree with the fact pack and not reveal everything immediately.

Evaluate: test ordinary questions, vague questions, and attempts to override the rules. Record expected and actual behavior, corrections, and retest results.

Use only tools and data approved for the class. A public report is not a license to use confidential records or imply access to unpublished audit workpapers.

Common issues to test include fabricated facts, unintended disclosure, inconsistent reveal behavior, loss of instructions during long conversations, and memory carryover between runs.

AI may support practice and drafting, but it does not take responsibility for professional judgment or conclusions.

No coding required: the assignment focuses on designing and configuring a reusable assistant workspace. Confirm the platform your course approves.

Use fictional data: remove or invent people, organizations, systems, vendors, dates, IDs, amounts, and documents.

Errors are test findings: document the prompt, the incorrect behavior, the desired behavior, the change made, and the result after retesting.

A successful project begins with a narrow process and a clear learning objective. Build only enough detail to make the interview realistic and the evidence testable.

Evaluation should reward observable interviewing behavior—process understanding, follow-up questions, control identification, evidence requests, and clear closeout—not simply guessing the issue.
